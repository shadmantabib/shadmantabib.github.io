## Prelude

<p class="journey-lede">I am typing this from nearly 8,000 miles away from my very own home—the everyday space where I belonged for 25 long years. Sometimes it feels dreamy; sometimes it feels like a strange combination of seclusion and solitude. Above everything, I feel extremely blessed to be here, and grateful to the Almighty.</p>

I received many notes asking me to share my Ph.D. journey and application process. I had been extremely lazy about it, partly because I never felt that I was accomplished enough to give “advice” to others. But now I am finally wrapping up everything I faced from end to end, beginning with my slightly **“lunatic” decision** to apply for Fall 2026 while I was still an undergraduate.

<div class="journey-note"><strong>A necessary disclaimer.</strong> This is a record of my own experience applying mainly to highly ranked CS Ph.D. programs in the United States. Admissions practices differ across universities, departments, faculty members, and application cycles. Treat my observations as personal impressions—not universal rules or official admissions policy.</div>

## How It Started

My Level 4, Term 1 finals at BUET ended in the second week of November. I sat silently at my table and began laying out every plan I could imagine for the next six or seven months, when I would hopefully graduate. I sorted the possibilities, weighed their pros and cons, and scored them against my present portfolio.

On **16 November**, I decided to apply for Fall ’26.

Life had been too harsh for the previous four years, so I found myself thinking: *what else should I lose except money?*

The decision immediately created a problem. I needed to sit for IELTS as soon as possible. With university deadlines approaching, the window was extremely narrow. I booked the exam for **19 November**, leaving myself only two or three days to prepare. It was a deadlock situation—and, honestly, a gamble with luck.

I dared to sit for it anyway.

<blockquote class="pull-quote">Three days separated the decision from the test. Sometimes a journey begins before you have time to feel ready.</blockquote>

## IELTS, TOEFL, and GRE

Most universities require international applicants to demonstrate English proficiency through IELTS or TOEFL. In the United States, both are widely accepted, although requirements vary by university, program, and even by individual section: Reading, Listening, Speaking, and Writing.

I chose IELTS because of its broader portability across countries, even though I eventually applied only in the United States. In general, strong Reading and Listening scores make it easier to raise the overall band. A higher Speaking score can also make teaching-assistant eligibility easier at some universities. Still, there are often ways to meet TA requirements after admission, so I would not describe any single speaking score as an absolute rule.

I had shared my IELTS resources with a friend, who later organized them into a useful guide. You can find it [here](https://lnkd.in/p/g_aZP-up). Because my preparation time was so short, I could not properly practice the Writing structure and barely crossed the margin I needed for Ph.D. admission.

But the gamble worked this time.

As for the **GRE**, it was optional at every school to which I applied, so I did not take it. I cannot comment confidently on programs where it may be mandatory. I have also heard of applicants using a strong GRE score to compensate for concerns about CGPA, but I did not experience that first-hand.

## Letters of Recommendation

I received my IELTS result on **21 November**. Once I knew I was eligible to apply to my intended schools, I turned immediately to letters of recommendation—the part I consider among the most important in the entire application.

I contacted professors with whom I had worked closely. During my undergraduate years, I collaborated remotely with **Dr. Min Xu** at Carnegie Mellon University and **Dr. Ben Lengerich** at the University of Wisconsin–Madison. I believed they could write highly specific letters grounded in our work together. I also requested letters from three professors in CSE at BUET: **Dr. M. Sohel Rahman**, **Dr. Md. Shamsuzzoha Bayzid**, and **Dr. A.B.M. Alim Al Islam**.

The famous name on a letter matters less than many applicants imagine if the letter itself is superficial. A low-confidence or generic recommendation from a globally renowned professor can backfire. What matters is whether that professor knows your work well enough to write about you in a detailed, **niche** manner: your particular contributions, your research habits, how you handle difficulty, and how you work with others.

Most U.S. Ph.D. programs I encountered required at least three letters. I sometimes chose different recommenders for different programs. If I applied to a program strong in Health AI and named faculty in that domain in my SoP, I tried to include recommenders who had supervised my Health AI work. There is no hard-and-fast rule here; I followed my intuition about alignment.

A few practical points from my experience:

- A professor may ask you for a first draft and then revise it independently, sometimes after consulting co-PIs from the project. You cannot see the final version submitted to the portal.
- Portals generally ask whether you waive your right to inspect the letter. My strong recommendation is to waive access by selecting **“Yes.”** Committees often treat waived letters as more candid and credible.
- Send recommendation requests through the portals early. Your recommenders need time, and you do not have to finish the entire application before inviting them.
- Some portals ask recommenders for ratings in addition to the written letter. This makes it even more important to ask people who can assess you confidently.

## Statement of Purpose

After arranging my recommendation letters, I began writing a separate Statement of Purpose for each university. The SoP is another central part of the application.

The fewer unrealistic and vague statements you make, the more convincing the document becomes. Your motivation for research should connect from beginning to end, revolving around one central idea throughout your journey. That was my strongest impression while applying.

**Do not involve multiple unrelated domains at once.** It can make your Ph.D. interests appear unfocused.

The most irritating part of the SoP was identifying professors at every university whose work genuinely aligned with mine. I had to go manually through department and faculty websites and prepare those lists. Yes, I know what you are thinking: it would be a thousand times easier if a web agent could sort prospective professors for each university. It is very possible now, and you can try it before you start applying. I simply did not have the time.

For reference, I am sharing the [SoPs I prepared during my application season](https://www.overleaf.com/read/tckrvgsqrfqf#21cce8). I did not ultimately apply to every university included there, but the collection may help you understand how I shaped the flow for different programs.

## Mailing Professors

This topic confuses many applicants because the value of emailing professors depends heavily on the program.

You will often hear about two broad admissions models: **professor-driven** and **committee-based (or central)**. In the first, an individual professor or principal investigator has substantial influence, often tied to available funding. In the second, a faculty committee performs an initial review before applications reach potential advisors.

Most programs I applied to appeared to be committee-based. From conversations after arriving at UIUC, I came to understand the central process as a long sequence: a very large pool is screened, a smaller group receives detailed faculty review, promising applications are circulated to faculty in the relevant research area, interviews follow, and recommendations eventually return to the graduate admissions leadership. This is my informal understanding—not an official description of UIUC’s process.

Professor-driven programs may involve fewer layers, with a PI’s research fit and funding capacity playing a more direct role. Regardless of the formal model, I think it is reasonable to email professors before applying. It may matter less in central admissions, but it can be crucial in professor-driven programs.

I had mostly chosen central programs and started preparing emails only around **25 November**, painfully close to deadlines beginning on 1 December. If I could repeat the process, I would prepare a list of professors and email addresses much earlier.

### How can you tell which model a program uses?

There is no single open book that tells you. You may get hints from how faculty respond to prospective-student emails, whether students rotate through labs, and how the department describes advisor matching. Based only on my application experience, I suspected that the CS admissions processes at schools such as MIT, Stanford, CMU, UIUC, Cornell, Princeton, USC, UW–Madison, the University of Washington, NYU, Georgia Tech, Harvard, Johns Hopkins, and Duke leaned central or committee-based.

Again, **do not treat this as an absolute list**. Departments change, and different programs within one university can work very differently.

### The email structure I followed

My emails were direct: I introduced myself, focused quickly on my publication record and research alignment with the professor, and attached or linked my latest résumé, SoP, and website.

## CV, Résumé, and Website

There are no hard-and-fast rules for building a CV, résumé, or academic website, but each plays a meaningful role. One of the first things a PI may inspect is the applicant’s résumé.

A CV and résumé are not quite the same. A résumé is a focused, two-to-two-and-a-half-page summary of your portfolio; a CV is a longer and more comprehensive record. For applications, I generally preferred a concise résumé. An admissions committee has a bulk of files to review, and an unnecessarily long document can distract from your key strengths.

You can view [my application résumé here](https://drive.google.com/file/d/17qb9xphHRIcA3jv6nbaUDoFQzLIWipDP/view?usp=sharing).

I would keep the portfolio website simple as well. Academics often prefer a plain, clear structure that lets them find research, publications, and contact details immediately. I visited many faculty websites and noticed that most followed a modest base theme. My own site, of course, is the one you are reading now.

## The Deadline Sprint: 1–15 December

I tried to complete and collect every major component before **30 November**. Somehow, I finished on exactly 30 November. Then I began opening portals and submitting applications.

I focused mostly on highly ranked universities in the United States. To understand the landscape, I consulted both [U.S. News graduate CS rankings](https://www.usnews.com/best-graduate-schools/top-computer-science-schools/computer-science-rankings) and [CSRankings](https://csrankings.org/#/index?all&us). Their methodologies are very different: U.S. News combines multiple institutional measures and reputation surveys, while CSRankings is based on faculty publications at selected venues. I treated rankings as one input, not as a substitute for research fit.

In total, I applied to roughly **18 universities**, mostly through central or committee-based processes. Remember that a single university may host several graduate programs with very different research emphases and acceptance patterns. CMU’s School of Computer Science, for example, includes programs spanning machine learning, computer science, HCI, and computational biology. Health or computational-biology programs may also ask for grades in relevant biology or health courses.

I encountered no portal that specifically asked for class rank except CMU’s CPCB program. Most requested the overall CGPA and transcript.

Be cautious with time: completing one university application properly can take **four or five hours, sometimes more**. Leave enough space before the deadline to review every field and document.

Once the last application is submitted, take a breath and relax.

The intense waiting—and suffocation—is about to begin. I am sorry to say it like that, but that is what it is.

## The Interviews: Mid-January to Mid-March

The first hell phase was the waiting: recklessly refreshing my inbox and spam folder again and again. The most horrific part was that all my undergraduate classes, labs, exams, and other responsibilities were continuing at the same time.

For the central programs, an interview invitation may come from the university, a lead PI, or a professor whose lab you named. Waiting for that invaluable email—the first real sign of a probable destination—was extremely suffocating.

Then came the interviews themselves.

In my experience, the first round usually introduced the PI to my background, research experience, and publications. If the conversation went well, a second round could become much less straightforward. I was sometimes given research papers from the lab or from top-tier venues and expected to discuss them deeply in the next meeting. I prepared slides, studied the assumptions and methods, and tried to anticipate every possible question.

Whatever the difficulty, you should be able to answer with confidence and honesty. Superficial knowledge will not carry you through a technical interview.

I will allow myself one small flex: after reaching UIUC, I was told that I had answered one interview question that none of the other candidates had even fully understood.

An occasional third round may ask for a research idea aligned with the PI’s lab, although this was rare for me. Most of my interview processes involved around two rounds, but there is no universal pattern. The structure varies by program and PI.

I am sharing [one set of slides from my interviews](https://docs.google.com/presentation/d/1tLCLivcMS3QY9h0k2s0odmErUiydNjToa8-lcCeW58Y/edit?usp=sharing) for reference.

### What earns an interview?

Fortunately or unfortunately, that remains a black box. My personal guess was:

**LoR ≥ Publications > CGPA > Competitions > Other extracurricular activities**

That is only my stipulation. I received roughly **15 interview rounds** across MIT, UIUC, UMD College Park, Dartmouth, USC, UVA, UC Riverside, and UMass Amherst. I was fortunate, and I feel blessed by the Almighty. In my case, substantial research experience, a decent CGPA, publications, and very strong recommendations from faculty at highly ranked universities probably mattered most.

<div class="journey-note"><strong>On undergraduate versus master’s applicants.</strong> I do not believe undergraduates are automatically prioritized over master’s students with stronger research experience. There are logical trade-offs. A master’s applicant may be expected to show one or two strong publications, but an undergraduate with no publications is not inherently favored over a master’s graduate who has them. If you demonstrate the same research maturity earlier in your career, that can make you unusually competitive—but the comparison is never that simple.</div>

## The Decision

Final decisions arrived from late February through mid-April. By then, I was so exhausted that I sometimes thought I would never apply in the United States again if I did not reach one of the schools I hoped for. The process took a serious toll on my physical and mental health.

At the same time, I was organizing **DL Sprint 4.0** for CS Fest while keeping up with class tests, assignments, and regular university classes. I worked harder than I knew I could. I wish I carried that kind of ability throughout my life. Most importantly, I was blessed by the grace of the Almighty to hold everything together at once.

The situation for international students felt especially difficult that year, and decisions seemed to arrive later. I waited, waited, and entered what felt like a never-ending loop.

From my UIUC interview, I had received hints that an offer might come.

Then, on **16 March**, I received a rejection from UIUC.

It was the 27th night of Ramadan—Laylat al-Qadr. I felt frozen from feet to head. There was nothing I could do except pray to the Almighty.

After a while came an almost unbelievable message: the previous email had been sent by mistake, and my application would be placed under review again.

So another cycle of waiting began. This time, I barely had the strength to carry the stress. I simply waited for the days to pass by any means.

Finally, on **Chand Raat**, the night before Eid, the precious email arrived: I had been accepted to the **Siebel School of Computing and Data Science at the University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign**.

<blockquote class="pull-quote">I did not scream with joy. I went quiet for a few days, told my parents, and let the truth settle in: that day had finally come.</blockquote>

Gratitude to the Almighty.

The days afterward were smoother, except for the stretch between my visa interview and approval. I later received offers from UC Riverside and the University of Virginia, which I declined after choosing UIUC.

I feel heavenly blessed to have drawn a dreamy conclusion to this “pathetic” journey.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Do grades really matter in CS Ph.D. admissions?

I am not completely sure. For highly ranked, committee-based programs, I suspect grades may be used as an early screening signal, but I cannot verify a universal cutoff. In professor-driven programs, CGPA may matter less when an applicant demonstrates strong research experience. Either way, do not assume grades are irrelevant; they are one part of a broader research profile.

### What kind of research experience matters most?

For AI/ML-oriented CS applications, a main-track publication at venues such as CVPR, NeurIPS, ICML, ICLR, or ICCV can boost an application enormously. Strong Q1 journal publications also matter. From my interviews, I felt that one excellent, highly relevant paper can sometimes make an applicant more competitive than several minor or weakly aligned publications.

But this is not absolute. Venue, authorship, contribution, recommendation letters, research fit, opportunity, and luck all interact.

### Should I complete a master’s in Bangladesh first, or apply during undergrad?

It depends on the competency already present in your portfolio. There is always a trade-off between applying early and using a master’s degree to build deeper preparation. The right choice depends on your research readiness, life priorities, and personal timeline.

My preference was to begin the Ph.D. early. I knew I wanted it for the research, and I felt that moving forward while still in the flow of study would be most convenient for me.

## Hope

I began this process thinking I might lose little more than an application fee. I ended it 8,000 miles from the place I had called home for 25 years.

This is not a blueprint, and certainly not a promise that the same choices will produce the same outcome. It is simply the most honest account I can give: prepare early if you can, work closely enough with people that they can speak about you with conviction, know your research beyond the surface, and leave room for uncertainty.

Somewhere between a three-day IELTS gamble, eighteen applications, fifteen interview rounds, a mistaken rejection, prayer on Laylat al-Qadr, and an acceptance on Chand Raat, the impossible became my next address.

**Alhamdulillah—and onward.**
